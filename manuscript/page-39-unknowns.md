## Unknown unknowns: the history of surprise

Our forecasts (p 7, p 37) describe *planned* discoveries. History insists on a correction: the biggest finds were rarely sought ::est:: [R20].

### Surprise case files

- **Pulsars (1967).** Radio astronomers tracking "scintillation" found a 1.33-second tick — initially nicknamed LGM-1 ("little green men") as a joke. It was a rotating neutron star: a whole new class of object ::est::.
- **The microwave background (1964–65).** Two engineers calibrating a horn antenna measured a stubborn noise excess — checked antennas, cleaned pigeon droppings — and had found the Big Bang's afterglow ::est:: [R20].
- **Dark energy (1998).** Survey teams measuring cosmic *deceleration* found acceleration instead — the instruments were built for one question and answered a different one ::est:: [R08].
- **Fast radio bursts (2007).** Found in *archived* data while looking for pulsars; explained only over the following decade(s) ◆ [R17].

**Going deeper.** Pattern ::est::: surprise favours (1) new instruments, (2) archival re-analysis, (3) anomaly-tolerant minds. All three are affordable to a curious reader — instruments via open data [R17], archives via public datasets, minds via p 24's discipline.

**Advanced corner.** Statistical humility: our 2050 forecast's biggest error term is not the known unknowns (biosignature ambiguity, dark sector) — it's the category that doesn't have a name yet ◆.
