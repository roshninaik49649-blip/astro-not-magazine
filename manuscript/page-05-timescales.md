## 24 years vs 13.8 billion

### The timescale shock

Put the two numbers side by side and the answer starts writing itself:

| Interval | Length | What happens in it |
|---|---|---|
| 13.8 billion years | age of the Universe ::est:: | galaxies assembled; the Sun formed; continents drifted |
| 1 million years | — | the Sun barely changes; species evolve |
| 24 years (2026→2050) | 0.0000002 % of cosmic time | ~2 % of the Sun's spot cycle; a blink ::est:: |

The Planck 2018 results pin the age of the Universe at 13.8 billion years with sub-percent precision [R05]. On that clock, 24 years is invisible.

### What the graph shows

Our capability chart (above) compresses telescope technology into one log-scale line. Two honest warnings: the axis is logarithmic — doubling looks flat — and everything after 2026 is an **extrapolation** ▲, marked as such. Instruments can improve enormously by 2050; the Universe they observe will not.

**Going deeper.** The closest cosmic "clock hands" to human timescales are solar activity (~11-year cycles) and asteroid orbits (hours to years). But even violent events — supernovae — light up once per galaxy per century, on average ::est::, and don't transform the Universe when they happen elsewhere.

**Advanced corner.** Cosmologists quantify "drastic" with dimensionless parameters (H₀, Ω_m, Ω_Λ). Between 2013 and 2026 our *measurements* of these shifted at the percent level — while the *parameters themselves* are constant to far better precision ::est:: [R05].
