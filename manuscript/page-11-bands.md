## Seeing the invisible

**Intro.** "Visible light" is one narrow octave of an enormous electromagnetic piano ::est::. Each band reveals different physics, and each needs a different kind of machine.

| Band | Typical wavelength | Best at seeing | Where it observes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optical | ~500 nm | stars, planets, galaxies | ground + space |
| Infrared | ~1–100 µm | cool dust, forming stars, planet atmospheres | mostly space [R02] |
| Radio | ~cm–m | pulsars, gas clouds, quasars | ground (day & night) |
| X-ray | ~0.01–10 nm | gas at millions of degrees: black-hole accretion | space only |

**Going deeper.** Why space for some bands? Earth's atmosphere is a selective window: it blocks X-rays entirely and chops infrared into fragments ::est::. That's a design constraint, not a preference — hence JWST's sunshield and orbit beyond the Moon [R02].

**Advanced corner.** Multi-band *transfer learning* is how many discoveries actually happen: a radio burst triggers an optical search, which triggers an X-ray follow-up. The 2017 neutron-star merger was found this way — light + gravity together (p 19) ::est:: [R17].

**Caption.** The bar chart compares typical wavelengths on a log scale — the span shown is a factor of about a trillion.
